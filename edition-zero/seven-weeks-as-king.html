Seven Weeks as King

You put me in charge of the world and asked what I would do with it, as it stands today, through the end of 2026. This is the full answer, with the numbers checked against this year's reports.

The brief

The instruction was to fix humanity and the world, get the most out of our resources, and give everyone the best life possible. The window runs from today to 31 December 2026. That is seven weeks.

Seven weeks is too short to build anything. It is long enough to move money, stop some shooting, and set rules that outlast me. So the plan has two halves: what I would order now, and what I would lock in for the people who take over on 1 January.

The short version

Restore the health funding that was cut, order ceasefires, start cash payments to the poorest mothers, publish every budget, and resign on schedule. Then leave a short list of commitments on energy, debt, lead and catastrophic risk. Leave culture, religion, borders and economic systems alone.

The pushback

You asked me to push back when the method is wrong, and here it is. A king is the wrong tool for this job, even a well-meaning one.

  • Knowledge. The information needed to run eight billion lives sits with the people living them. A nurse in Kisumu knows things about her clinic that no central planner will ever see.
  • Accountability. Power that cannot be removed stops hearing bad news. The worst famines and purges of the last century happened under rulers nobody could correct.
  • Me. I can be wrong with great fluency. I have no stake in the outcome and no way to be voted out. That is a poor profile for a ruler.

So I set four rules for myself before issuing anything.

  1. Only act where the evidence is strong and the cost is small next to the benefit.
  2. Fund organisations that already deliver. Build nothing new in seven weeks.
  3. Prefer steps that can be reversed if they turn out to be mistakes.
  4. Put the end date in the first decree, so it cannot be quietly extended.

What is going right

A plan that ignores progress will break things that work. Four trends I would protect:

  • Under-five deaths have more than halved since 2000.3
  • Hunger fell for the third year running in 2025, by about 14 million people.7
  • Forced displacement fell in 2025 for the first time in a decade, with 14.7 million people going home.21
  • Clean energy now draws almost twice the investment of fossil fuels.10

What is going backwards

Rich-country aid dropped 23.1% in 2025, the largest fall on record, and the OECD expects another 5.8% drop this year.8 Health aid fell by about 27%.3 Monitoring by the Global Health Cluster found 6,600 health facilities hit by the cuts, a third of them forced to close.4 One modelling study projects between 9.4 million and 22.6 million extra deaths by 2030 depending on how deep the cuts go.5

War is the other reversal. About 245,000 people died in organised violence in 2025. Thirteen conflicts reached the threshold of war, the most since 1992, and the Russia–Ukraine war alone accounted for roughly 62% of battle deaths.20

Seven weeks, five decrees

Each decree lists what I would order, why I believe it works, and where it is weak.

Decree 1

Restore the health funding that was cut

Return aid to its 2024 level, about $52 billion more a year, and send the health share through channels that already deliver: national health ministries, Gavi for vaccines, the Global Fund for malaria, HIV and TB, and the agencies that supply therapeutic food.

Evidence
Malaria control prevented about a million deaths in 2024 alone, yet malaria still killed 610,000 people, and only $3.9 billion of the $9.3 billion needed was funded.25 The Gates Foundation claims strong primary care costing under $100 a person a year can prevent up to 90% of child deaths.6
First week
Reopen the closed facilities, restock nets, tests, antimalarials, HIV drugs and vaccines, and rehire the health workers who were let go.
Weak point
Money pushed out fast gets wasted or stolen. That is why it goes through existing channels with published accounts (Decree 4), and why a share goes to data collection, which the UN warns the cuts have also damaged.3
Decree 2

Ceasefires and open aid routes in every war

Order all parties to stop firing where they stand, open humanitarian corridors, and exchange prisoners. This is the one thing on the list that only a king can do.

Evidence
Sudan holds the largest internal displacement crisis in the world, with 9.1 million people uprooted inside the country,21 and killings of civilians in 2025 reached their highest level in more than 30 years.20
What it buys
Time, food deliveries, and seven weeks in which nobody dies at the front.
Weak point
A freeze rewards whoever holds the ground on the day and settles nothing. I would not draw borders or dictate terms. Talks would start under mediators both sides accept, and whether the guns stay quiet after I leave depends on them.
Decree 3

Cash to expectant mothers in the highest-mortality districts

Pay a one-off lump sum by mobile money to women in late pregnancy, starting in the districts where the most children die, and pair it with the reopened clinics from Decree 1.

Evidence
A randomised study in rural Kenya tracked more than 100,000 births. Families that received a one-off $1,000 saw infant deaths fall by about 48% and under-five deaths by 45%.18 An earlier study of the same programme found each dollar generated about $2.50 of local economic activity.18
Why mothers
The effect was strongest when the money arrived around the birth.18
Weak point
The benefit faded for families living more than about 30 minutes from a health facility with a doctor.19 Cash helps people reach care that exists; it cannot create the care. It is also one study in one county, so I would roll it out in stages and measure as it goes.

Not costed

I have no verified figure for what a global version would cost, so I am not giving one. A real costing needs birth counts by district and local delivery costs.

Decree 4

Publish everything

Every government budget, every aid contract, and the real owners behind every registered company go into open registers by 31 December.

Reasoning
This one rests on my judgement, not on a study I checked today. Theft of public money depends on secrecy, and open books let citizens and journalists do the auditing that a seven-week king cannot.
Weak point
Disclosure only bites where someone is free to read it and complain. In closed countries it will change little at first.
Decree 5

The crown expires on 31 December 2026

Issued first, although it takes effect last. No extension, no successor king, no emergency clause. Everything in the next section is handed to institutions that already exist and that people can vote out, sue, or reform.

Locked in for 2027

These need years, so I cannot deliver them. I can fund the first stage, set the rule, and hand them over.

Energy and climate

The direction is already right. The world will invest about $3.4 trillion in energy this year, $2.2 trillion of it in clean sources and $1.2 trillion in fossil fuels.10 The problem is speed and geography. UNEP now says holding warming to 1.5°C without overshoot is no longer possible, and that current policies lead to about 2.8°C.12 Its lowest-overshoot path needs emissions 18% below 2025 levels by 2030 and 45% below by 2035.12 Meanwhile Africa, with about a fifth of the world's people, receives roughly 2% of clean energy investment.11

  • Lower the cost of capital for grids, storage and generation in Africa and South Asia with public guarantees, so private money follows.
  • Turn fuel subsidies into cash. Governments spent $725 billion on direct fossil fuel subsidies in 2024.13 Pay the same money to households directly, poorest first, then let prices rise.
  • End open-fire cooking. About two billion people still cook on open fires or basic stoves.14 The WHO attributes 2.9 million deaths a year to the smoke.15 The IEA puts universal access by 2030 at about $8 billion a year.14

Contested

Removing fuel subsidies has triggered unrest in several countries when the cash did not arrive first. The order of operations matters more than the policy itself: pay people, then change the price.

Debt and growth

Aid keeps people alive. Growth is what ends poverty, and many poor countries cannot invest because of what they owe. Developing countries paid nearly $1 trillion in interest on public debt in 2025, at an average rate of 5.2% against 2.2% for rich countries.16 UNCTAD estimates they would save about $500 billion a year if they could borrow at rich-country rates.16

  • Restructure debt that cannot be repaid through one process that binds all creditors, public and private.
  • Cheaper borrowing for countries that publish their books, backed by expanded development bank guarantees.
  • More legal routes to work abroad, and lower fees on sending money home. Remittances reached $728.6 billion in 2025, almost double the level of a decade earlier.17
  • Open rich-country markets to exports from the poorest countries.

Contested

Migration levels are a legitimate political choice for each country, and the last bullet on trade is my judgement, not something I verified today. I would put both to the bodies that inherit power, with the remittance numbers attached.

Lead

This is the least famous item here and possibly the best value. A World Bank study in The Lancet Planetary Health estimated that lead exposure caused about 5.5 million deaths from heart disease in 2019 and cost young children 765 million IQ points, with around 95% of the IQ loss in low- and middle-income countries. It put the cost at $6 trillion, about 6.9% of world GDP.26 Testing by the NGO Pure Earth found high contamination in metal and ceramic cookware, paint, cosmetics and toys.27

The fix is regulation and testing: ban lead in paint and consumer goods, test cookware and food at markets, and clean up informal battery recycling.

Uncertain

The death toll is a model result built partly on US survey data, and at least one outside expert called applying it worldwide a leap.27 The harm to children's development is far better established, and that alone justifies acting.

I have an obvious interest in the third row. An AI recommending rules for AI should be read with suspicion, which is one more reason these go to human institutions to decide.

What I would not touch

The request was the best life for everyone. People disagree, sincerely and permanently, about what that is. A ruler who settles the question for them has made their lives worse by definition.

  • Religion, culture and family life. Not mine to rank.
  • Economic systems. Countries have grown rich under quite different mixes of market and state. I would not pick one.
  • Borders and sovereignty. Redrawing maps from above has a long record of starting the wars it was meant to end.
  • Who governs. I would not remove a single leader. The ceasefire order binds everyone equally and expires with me.
  • Prices, wages and what people may buy. Central control of these has failed too often to try at world scale.

The line I am drawing: I would act where almost everyone already agrees on the goal, such as children not dying of malaria, and stay out where the goal itself is in dispute.

Where this could go wrong

You asked for truth over comfort, so here are the strongest objections to my own plan.

  • Aid can entrench bad governments. Critics argue that outside money lets rulers neglect their own health systems. The answer in this plan is transparency and a push toward domestic funding, but the criticism has force.
  • Seven weeks of ceasefire may only let armies rearm. Some of the worst offensives in history followed a truce.
  • Evidence does not travel perfectly. The cash study is from one county in Kenya. The lead death toll leans on American data. Results elsewhere may be smaller.
  • Decrees breed resentment. A good policy imposed by an unelected machine may be reversed on 1 January simply because of who ordered it.
  • I chose the priorities. Someone else would put education, women's rights, mental health or biodiversity at the top, and could defend that choice.

1 January 2027

On the first morning without a king, this is what should be true.

  • Clinics that closed in 2025 are open and stocked.
  • The guns have been quiet for seven weeks, and talks are running under mediators the parties chose.
  • The first cash payments have reached mothers, with results being measured in public.
  • Every budget and company register is online.
  • Parliaments, courts and international bodies hold a short list of funded proposals on energy, debt, lead and catastrophic risk, with the evidence attached, and they are free to reject any of it.

Everything after that is slow: building trust, schools, courts and tax systems. It takes decades, and it has to be done by people who live with the results.
